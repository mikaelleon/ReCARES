## **Abstract**

Camella Homes Tibig, a gated subdivision in Lipa City, Batangas, currently manages community safety and resident services through a single walk-in office and a set of personal social media accounts, a model that leaves victims of violence against women and children without a discreet way to report abuse, denies residents with disabilities and mobility limitations any means of completing a transaction without a physical office visit, and gives residents living near the perimeter wall no rapid channel for flagging a security threat. This study proposes ReCARES (Resident Centered Assistance for Reporting and Emergency System), a web-based subdivision community safety, resident services, and incident reporting platform that will provide a discreet emergency alert feature grounded in Republic Act No. 9262 and Republic Act No. 7610, role-based access control across six defined user roles, namely Resident, Security Personnel, Homeowners Association Staff, Case Handler, Homeowners Association Admin, and System Admin, an artificial intelligence layer restricted to suggesting a category, with household linking and confidential routing handled by application rules, and a digital service request and tracking system that removes the need for an in-person office visit. The study will adopt Design Science Research as its overall research design and the Agile Software Development Life Cycle as its construction method, gathering data through a needs assessment questionnaire and interview guide administered to residents, security personnel, and Homeowners Association staff, followed by Developer Functional Testing and User Acceptance Testing evaluated against the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model and the System Usability Scale. The platform will be proposed and implemented for the Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners Association as its primary client, and its findings will apply specifically to this subdivision within the academic year 2026 to 2027.

**Keywords:** community safety, violence against women and children, PWD accessibility, role-based access control, discreet emergency alert, homeowners association, incident reporting

## **1.1 Project Context**

```
Violence against women and children is one of the most serious human rights concerns worldwide, yet decades of prevention efforts remain inadequate. The United Nations defined the issue formally through its Declaration on the Elimination of Violence Against Women in 1993, describing it as any act of gender-based violence that results in physical, sexual, or psychological harm, whether it happens in public or within a person's own home (as cited in Consignado et al., 2022). More than thirty years later, the problem has not gone away. Bando et al. (2024) mention the 2021 global data from the World Health Organization, in which it reported that gender-based violence still affects one in three women in their lifetime. Most of it is inflicted by an intimate partner, and children face a related and often overlapping pattern of physical, sexual, and psychological abuse within the same households. (Puno et al., 2023\) The same decades-long push for international recognition extends to persons with disabilities, whose right to reach basic services on equal terms with everyone else has become an increasingly recognized global principle, although most public and private systems were still, in the present time, not built with that access in mind. (Aguilar, n.d.) The two concerns remain widely recognized on paper, but inconsistently enforced once they move from international agreements into the daily reality of individual countries, communities, and persons. 

This gap between international recognition and local enforcement is prominent in the Philippines, where a comprehensive set of national laws already exists on paper to address both problems. Consignado et al. (2022) identified Republic Act No. 9262, the Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act, as the anchor of the country's VAWC legal framework, which criminalizes abuse committed by a spouse, partner, or parent and requires local government units to build support systems for victims. Consignado et al. (2022) further examined Republic Act No. 9710, the Magna Carta of Women, a law that requires every barangay to operate a VAW desk, which functions as a localized, gender-responsive channel through which victims can report abuse without having to travel outside their community. A separate statute, Republic Act No. 7610, the Special Protection of Children Against Abuse, Exploitation and Discrimination Act, addresses the same underlying problem for a different population: it explicitly protects children against abuse, exploitation, and neglect (Republic of the Philippines, 1992). As a result, no coverage gap remains between adult and minor victims living under the same roof. Furthermore, Republic Act No. 7277, the Magna Carta for Disabled Persons, requires the government and private institutions to make their services physically and practically accessible (Republic of the Philippines, 1992). Considered together, these four laws establish a comprehensive theoretical framework for protection. However, national data collected since their enactment show that this legal protection is not applied uniformly and remains heavily dependent on a victim’s socioeconomic status.

National statistics demonstrate that legal frameworks alone cannot overcome the systemic barriers preventing victims from seeking help. Valdez et al. (2022) found that one in four Filipino women has experienced gender-based violence. Of these women, forty-one percent never sought help at all. Valdez et al. (2022) partly attributed the women’s silence to patriarchal family structures. The first barrier is that referral paths across health, legal, and social services are fragmented. A second barrier is the length of the judicial process; victims weigh the costs of filing and testifying, and the risk that delay gives an abuser time to retaliate, against the uncertain benefit of reporting at all. Clores et al. (2018) reviewed police blotter records in Malvar, Batangas, covering 2014 to 2016\. Their review found that victims reported abuse only after their children faced danger. Valdez et al. (2022) also tracked reporting during the COVID-19 lockdown: officially reported cases fell twenty-seven percent, while internet searches related to violence against women rose sixty-three percent over the same period. Kibtiah and Tuharea (2024) identify two drivers of the silence of victims: financial dependence and a fear of social shame, which persist even when a reporting channel is already available. In some cases, financial dependence increases the severity of this barrier, as women who lack financial independence cannot afford the time or expense associated with formally reporting their abuser and also face the risk of having their abuser find out when they enter a local VAW Desk, the main avenue the Government uses to intervene in cases of Gender-based Violence. Combined, these physical and social barriers ultimately determine the effectiveness of the Barangay VAW desk in delivering support to its target population.

Persons with disabilities face a parallel pattern of exclusion once government and emergency services move online, and this pattern holds across countries with very different levels of digital development. Regardless of the level of digital development within each country, the same trend applies to persons with disabilities. Majid (2026) examined e-government access between Indonesia, the Philippines, and Dubai through several electronic government accessibilities and found that even though Dubai has one of the most sophisticated digital infrastructures in the world, it scored very poorly in comparison to other countries for how it treated persons with disabilities during its initial design process. The conclusion drawn by Majid (2026) suggests that investment in technology alone does not guarantee that the system can be utilized by the populace it intends to serve. Ouaziz et al. (2026) also produced similar results as Majid by completing a systematic mapping study of 112 published works on e-government portal accessibility, pointing out that the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) remain the most widely referenced standards in e-government research. However, even though the WCAG has been referenced in a number of works related to e-government portal accessibility, the application of these same guidelines to e-government systems is inconsistent. Bokingkito et al. (2025) specifically highlighted this discrepancy within the Philippines and indicated that on average, about 53% of government websites did not comply with very basic web presence standards. 

Even in instances where government website scores were above average for accessibility, the lack of the availability of expanded online services, mobile accessibility, and transaction features prevented persons with disabilities from completing a transaction fully. In Kaul & Prasad's (2024) study of five different countries regarding how they measure accessibility performance, they noted that the Philippines had the lowest ranking as to accessibility performance in comparison to the other four countries in areas such as transport, health facilities, public buildings, and digital services. This illustrates that the exclusion of persons with disabilities documented in the above studies is not limited to web-based services, but extends to all channels, both physical and institutional, that a person with a disability may use. Siqueira et al. (2022) provided an additional view of why reporting of accessibility failures is rare among persons with disabilities. Their interviewees mentioned the following reasons for not reporting accessibility failures: disbelief in the complaints process, limited understanding of the laws protecting them, and the excessive formality of reporting failures. Taken together, Majid's (2026), Ouaziz et al. (2026), Bokingkito et al., and Siqueira et al. (2022) indicate that PWD-accessible digital channels cannot merely exist on paper or pass a compliance check. Rather, the proof of the PWD-accessibility of a digital channel is that it can perform the task that the resident requested and that it is easy enough to use that the resident has confidence that reporting failures is worthwhile.

Homeowner associations in gated subdivisions replicate this inaccessible service model despite functioning as independent governance structures. The typical homeowner association operates from a single physical office with fixed hours staffed by a small number of personnel handling fee collection, security, and disputes simultaneously. This administrative structure does not allow any flexibility for residents who need to safely report domestic abuse and instead require that they walk in to report the incident, which ultimately leaves many victims without an option. The security systems employed by subdivisions do not address the issues residents may be experiencing within their own homes, but rather focus solely on external threats. Individuals who reside in remote areas of the subdivision from the security checkpoint or perimeter wall will have no means by which to alert guards of an emergency until it is too late. Regarding the digital solutions that exist for barangay-level reporting of VAWC, Subiza et al. (2026) created an online case-filing system and mobile VAWC reporting for barangays, allowing victims to file a report and directly connect with emergency hotlines via phone. Furthermore, Balahadia et al. (2022) found that many victims in Laguna were able to report incidents of violence using this mobile reporting tool because it provided a private and protected means of obtaining assistance from their partner's observation through in-person reporting. Neither of these digital alternatives currently exists for the communities governed by homeowner associations in Lipa City, including Camella Homes Tibig.



Although Camella Homes Tibig's home owner association is active online, leveraging camellalipahoa.com and its social media accounts, residents of the community have experienced the same issues with governance and access to their neighborhood as non-residents. Resident's experience with their official website is through a static document repository built from templates rather than as an online transactional site; however, residents can download PDF versions of the association's documents, but they must print these documents off and deliver them to the office in person during normal business hours. The inability to complete the final transaction on the association's website conveys a sense of being modernized electronically, yet, the true barriers to the physical build process remain. Individuals with physical disabilities will receive no benefit, as the process requires that they possess the ability to physically transport themselves to the office. As such, the official Homeowners Association communication channels do not adequately provide access to visually impaired individuals, nor does it accommodate individuals who cannot travel to the office.

The operational and security risks associated with the inability of the association to manage its community presence through an official business page and instead using unverified personal Facebook accounts will multiply due to accessibility failures. The visibility of communications on the personal Facebook account is by default public. For example, the posting of public callouts for rule violations on this personal Facebook account eliminates the ability of victims of domestic violence to report the violation in a private and secure way. The informal management of community presence through the personal Facebook accounts provides a significant amount of technical risk and vulnerability because Meta's platforms often do not send notifications when switching between multiple personal accounts as an admin. Therefore, an admin who is managing a community presence on their personal Facebook account may miss an urgent message related to a perimeter breach or an abuse incident. Also, every contact point available on the website and on social media requires you to enter your full name, your phone number and your complete address onto a single shared form. This means that there is no separation between your routine maintenance and the sensitive nature of any security reports you make on the same form. Residents of the community are in a dangerous situation because the infrastructure of the association creates a façade of safety while virtually assuring that sensitive and urgent security-related information goes unnoticed. The proponents of this study propose a centralized web-based platform to resolve these established gaps in community safety and resident services.
```

## **1.2 Purpose and Description of the Project**

```
ReCARES is designed around three groups of Camella Homes Tibig residents whose needs the current service model fails most directly. Women and children experiencing violence cannot safely use a visible, in-person reporting channel without risking exposure to the person they are reporting. Residents with disabilities or mobility limitations cannot reach the Homeowners Association office during its limited hours to complete even a routine transaction. Residents in phases near the perimeter wall have no rapid way to report a break-in risk before it escalates into an actual intrusion. ReCARES aims to solve six core problems based on the situational analysis in Camella Homes Tibig, each of which traces back to one or more of these three groups.

ReCARES aims to solve six core problems based on the situational analysis in Camella Homes Tibig. The first issue is that there is no discreet way of reporting incidences of violence against women and children, meaning that victims may risk exposing themselves, and those staying within their household, to their abuser. Presently, the existing contact forms, as well as social media platforms, have made it mandatory for an individual to provide his name, phone number, and complete address, as specified in the block and lot prior to a concern being addressed; any highly sensitive report goes through the same form or a regular social media inbox used for regular complaints. Therefore, such reports are relayed to staff who possess no special authority and training for the handling of confidential matters. The secondary option is to visit the office in person, thus putting the victim in a situation where he/she has to appear and that does not leave room for confidentiality.

The second issue concerns the inaccessibility of services to residents with disabilities and mobility restrictions, as the office still requires residents to visit the office in person to submit forms and complete transactions, making the situation even worse, particularly for people facing difficulties in transportation and scheduling. Additionally, there is no way for the resident to authorize a trusted person to carry out all required operations on his or her behalf, which makes finding help impossible for all people relying on help from others.

The third issue is what the proponents of this study refer to as false digitization; although residents are able to download forms online, they still need to print out the downloaded document, fill it in by hand, and deliver it to the association’s office in person. This system creates a difficulty for all constituents involved: residents, security personnel, and employees of the Homeowners Association, as they all have to perform tasks that would have otherwise been eliminated in a fully online process. 

The fourth issue refers to the absence of an emergency line for urgent issues, in cases where residents report urgent issues after the closing time of the office, the issue may remain unaddressed until the office reopens. The gap creates a distinct problem for each participant of the subdivision operating process. Because of the lack of an efficient, recognized system of contacting someone once the office closes for the day, people living next to the fence who experience an urgent security problem have no way to reach someone to help them. While security personnel do remain at the site 24 hours a day, they have yet to have reliable electronic means of communication that allows them to receive messages from residents, which makes it impossible for residents to rely on the available manpower in case of any emergency situation such as fire, robbery, or other critical issues that can appear at any time unexpectedly.

The fifth issue concerns the unreliable communication channel used by the association, which uses a private Facebook account rather than a page to communicate with the homeowners. Residents do not have a way to confirm whether a message claiming to come from the association is authentic, therefore persons who do not have connections or have no knowledge of the personal account might not receive the necessary information, notifications, or announcements. Additionally, each HOA officer has his or her personal Facebook account and Meta platform does not allow being logged onto two accounts at the same time, which means that the officer checking own account will miss the notifications incoming for the association account and vice versa.

The last issue is that there are no defined processes in how the information received about sensitive incidents and maintenance requests will be processed because they are sent in the same portal to personnel who do not have authority to deal with them. Thus, this represents different issues for each of the groups that support day-to-day operations within the community for all residents. Residents who submit sensitive information cannot be sure that the person reviewing their submission is subject to any confidentiality requirements since the same means by which their reports are submitted also serves as a mechanism for submitting routine maintenance tickets that will be processed through general staff. Therefore, security personnel are left in a position of ambiguity when it comes to escalation procedures because those incidents that should go directly and privately to a designated case handler too often find their way into the same queue as unrelated requests, leaving nothing on the intake process identifying which is which. Similarly, staff members from Homeowners Associations are left in a precarious situation where individuals monitoring the shared form or inbox will read and process confidential reports for which they have no acceptable training or authorization to manage.

ReCARES (Resident Centered Assistance for Reporting & Emergency System) is a web-based platform designed specifically for the residents of Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners' Association based on the role of a homeowner. Once the homeowner has been verified remotely from uploaded proof of residency and a government ID, they can send a silent alert from a device that adult member registered themselves. On-duty Security Personnel receive the location and the timestamp only. A resident who chooses the confidential report type has that report routed by the application to the Case Handler. The Case Handler logs the intake and refers it to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911. That role does not mediate between household members and does not decide the legal outcome. Residents with disabilities or mobility limits can submit documents and service requests without an office visit. A resident may name one trusted adult to submit those routine requests on their behalf. That delegation does not cover confidential reports, and it does not let one adult register another adult's device. The artificial intelligence layer reads the report text and suggests a category. It does not look up household records, does not link cases, and does not choose the queue. Application code links a confidential report to an open file for the same household, and the route follows the report type the resident chose.

The benefit of the platform emerges from the Homeowners Association treating security and accessibility as core aspects of its operation rather than informal matters handled through a private Facebook chat or a casual conversation at the office front desk. Reports are documented, filed, and categorized consistently and digitally, allowing the board of directors to analyze actual records of recurring issues rather than relying on assumptions. This shift benefits the three groups the platform is built around in distinct ways. Women and children experiencing violence gain a channel that does not expose them at the moment they report. Residents with disabilities or mobility limitations gain access to Homeowners Association services that no longer depend on a physical office visit during limited hours. Residents near the perimeter wall gain a fast, direct way to flag a break-in risk the moment it occurs rather than waiting for the office to reopen.

The ReCARES platform itself is not similar to any other existing systems or solutions. Barangay violence against women and children desks depend heavily on the judgment and training of the officer on duty (Consignado et al., 2022), wherein the platform keeps a confidential report with an intake-and-referral Case Handler rather than leaving it in a general inbox. Artificial intelligence only suggests a category. Although mobile reporting tools already exist, such as the Online Case Filing System designed and developed by Subiza et al. (2026) and the Mobile Reporting Tool developed by Balahadia et al. (2022) for the residents of Laguna, neither were designed and created for use within a subdivision that is governed by a Homeowners Association (HOA). As such, there is not an available platform that can provide support to a community that resembles Camella Homes Tibig. Additionally, neither of the aforementioned tools provide a means for addressing the one-way communication problem that Camella Homes Tibig is currently experiencing.
```

## **1.3 Objectives of the Project**

```
The aim of this study is to propose and develop ReCARES, an integrated community safety and resident services platform, for the Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners Association. Specifically, it seeks to:
```

1. Analyze the concepts of security and role-based access control (RBAC), reporting protocols, geolocation-based data capture, automated category suggestion, web accessibility standards, and the Data Privacy Act (Republic Act No. 10173).
2. Design and develop ReCARES with the following features, each named for the people it serves:
   1. For residents facing violence at home: a discreet emergency alert that sends on-duty Security Personnel only the device location, or the registered phase and lot, and a timestamp.
   2. For those same residents: a confidential report type they choose, which the application routes to the Case Handler for intake and referral to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911.
   3. For Security Personnel, Homeowners Association Staff, the Case Handler, the Homeowners Association Admin, and the System Admin: role-based access so each role sees only its own work, and so the System Admin cannot open report content.
   4. For the Homeowners Association Admin: a dashboard on which artificial intelligence suggests a category only. Application code links a confidential report to an open file for the same household. The dashboard also shows monthly counts and a phase map of complaints, without case narrative.
   5. For residents with disabilities or mobility limits: online service requests, document release, and status tracking that do not require an office visit, including one named trusted adult for routine requests only, and device registration that each adult member completes for their own device.
   6. For every resident: screens built to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines adopted in the design.
3. Propose and implement the ReCARES platform to the Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners Association as the primary client.
4. To evaluate the developed platform's adherence to the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Model in Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Usability, Security, and Reliability, the last of these because the discreet alert is an emergency function.
5. To assess usability through User Acceptance Testing with the sample and success thresholds in Section 3.6, using the System Usability Scale, and including residents with disabilities or mobility limitations.

## **1.4 Scope and Limitations of the Project**

```
The study is focused on the design, development, and deployment of a web-based Subdivision Community Safety, Digital Services, and Incident Reporting Platform with a discreet emergency alert and an artificial intelligence component that suggests a category only, tailored specifically for Camella Homes Tibig in Lipa City, Batangas. The primary target end-users include the Residents, Security Personnel, and the Homeowners Association (HOA) staff involved in monitoring security, managing incident reports, and community records of the aforementioned subdivision. 

The platform functionalities are designed to accommodate the distinct operational needs of Residents, Security Personnel, and HOA staff within their respective roles. The platform also enforces Role-Based Access Control (RBAC) so that a confidential report, which the resident chooses as that type, stays with the Case Handler, while Security Personnel receive only the location and timestamp needed to dispatch a response to an emergency alert in progress. The Case Handler records intake and a referral to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911, and does not mediate or decide the outcome. Information retrieval and document processing for general office services are digitized so that residents who cannot visit the office can finish the transaction online. A resident may name one trusted adult for routine requests only. Each adult member registers their own device. Verification of a new account is remote: the HOA Admin compares the uploaded proof of residency and government ID with the household record, and the applicant does not appear at the office. Personal information is processed under Republic Act No. 10173 for the purpose of the alert, report, or request only. The System Admin configures accounts and does not read report content. The designated Case Handler must have training on Republic Act No. 9262 and Republic Act No. 7610, a named backup, and a conflict-of-interest rule that passes a file about the handler's own household or a relative to that backup. The development and implementation timeline of this study spans the academic year 2026 to 2027.

The study conducted does not intend to expand its scope either geographically or into other developments outside of Camella Homes Tibig; any findings, design decisions, or performance results reported will apply strictly to this single subdivision rather than to external Homeowners Associations in general. Additionally, the proponents of this study will not provide physical infrastructure and hardware procurement.

The platform will not include a financial module capable of processing online payments, collecting dues, or managing financial and billing transactions. Additionally, the platform does not support offline data operations, as it requires stable internet connection for both resident access and administrative monitoring. Furthermore, the platform is limited to software development and digital accessibility, therefore it cannot provide specialized assistive technology such as braille terminals, nor does it support users  with severe intellectual or psychological disabilities who lack the functional capacity to interact with a standard web application or computer device.
```

## **1.5 Definition of Terms**

```
For the purposes of this study, all terminology has been defined within the context of this study.
```

**Artificial Intelligence (AI) module.** Refers to the system component that reads the text of an incoming report and suggests a category. In this study the AI module does not look up household records, does not link case files, does not choose which role receives a report, and does not decide a case outcome. Routing follows the report type the resident chooses. Household lookup and linking are application code.

**Barangay VAW Desk.** Refers to the reporting process established by Republic Act No. 9710 as a gender-responsive system designed to provide immediate assistance to victims of VAWC without requiring victims to leave their communities. In this study, the Barangay VAW Desk is the existing government mandated process ReCARES intends to support at the subdivision level.

**Case Handler.** Refers to the designated person who receives a report only when the resident chose the confidential report type, logs that intake, and refers it to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911. The Case Handler does not mediate between household members and does not decide the legal outcome. The role requires training on Republic Act No. 9262 and Republic Act No. 7610, a named backup handler, and a conflict-of-interest rule: a handler whose own household or a relative is the subject passes the file to the backup and does not open it. The Case Handler is the only role that can read the contents of a confidential report.

**Discreet Emergency Alert System (SOS Alert).** Refers to a feature of ReCARES allowing verified residents to activate a silent emergency signal from a registered address and send their location to the on-duty security, without needing to report to the Homeowners Association in person and also without revealing through visual communication that they needed help.

**False Digitization.** Refers to a condition, as defined by the proponents of this study, in which a service appears to have been digitized because the forms needed to be completed are available on the internet. However, the resident still needs to print the form, fill it out by hand, and physically deliver the form to the association's office. Therefore, although the service appears to be modernized, the actual process of how the service is delivered remains the same as before.

**Functional Suitability.** Refers to one of the quality characteristics under the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Model, evaluating whether ReCARES provides the functions needed to satisfy stated and implied user needs when the platform is used under specified conditions.

**Reliability.** Refers to one of the quality characteristics under the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Model. In this study Reliability evaluates whether the discreet emergency alert completes and reaches on-duty Security Personnel with its location and timestamp, including under repeated use, because that function is an emergency channel.

**Global Positioning System (GPS).** Refers to the satellite-based technology that ReCARES uses to automatically capture and transmit a resident's location, or the pre-filled subdivision phase and lot number for registered users.

**Homeowners Association (HOA).** Refers to the organization responsible for governing a residential subdivision, managing community operations such as security, fee collection, and dispute resolution. In this study, the Homeowners Association refers specifically to the Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners Association, the primary client of ReCARES.

**Mobile Reporting Tool.** Refers to the mobile-based reporting system developed by Balahadia et al. (2022) for victims of violence in Laguna, it is referenced in this study as a current digital solution to violence against women and children, but one that is not intended for use in a subdivision managed by a Homeowners Association.

**Online Case Filing System.** Refers to the barangay-level online case-filing and mobile reporting platform for violence against women and children developed by Subiza et al. (2026), cited in the current study as an online alternative, albeit not for subdivisions such as Camella Homes Tibig.

**Persons with Disabilities (PWD).** Refers to individuals whose access to basic services on equal terms as others is guaranteed by Republic Act 7277; they comprise one of the three target end user groups that ReCARES is intended to serve, using accessible digital service channels that do not necessitate a physical visit to a ReCARES office.

**Republic Act No. 7277 (Magna Carta for Disabled Persons).** Refers to the Philippine law requiring both government and private sector entities to ensure that persons with disabilities are able to access all of their services in a manner that is physically and practically accessible. This legislation is included in the present study as a component of the legal foundation for ReCARES' PWD-accessible service objective.

**Republic Act No. 7610 (Special Protection of Children Against Abuse, Exploitation and Discrimination Act).** Refers to the Philippine law that explicitly protects children against abuse, exploitation, and neglect. This law has been mentioned in this study as part of the legal framework for the ReCARES incident reporting objective.

**Republic Act No. 9262 (Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act).** Refers to the Philippine law that criminalizes abuse committed by a spouse, partner, or parent against women and children and requires local government units to build support systems for victims. This act was used in this study to demonstrate the basis on which ReCARES will support its objective of reporting violence against women and children.

**Republic Act No. 9710 (Magna Carta of Women).** Refers to the Philippine law requiring every barangay to operate a VAW desk and establishes the legal basis for the localized reporting mechanism that ReCARES extends into the subdivision.

**Republic Act No. 10173 (Data Privacy Act of 2012).** Refers to the Philippine law that requires personal information to be collected for a declared purpose and kept from people who do not need it for that purpose. In this study it limits what each role can see: Security Personnel see a location and a timestamp, the Case Handler sees a confidential report the resident chose to send, and the System Admin does not see report content.

**Resident.** Refers to a verified homeowner or authorized household member of Camella Homes Tibig, who has registered to submit VAWC reports through ReCARES, request services and activate the discreet emergency alert system associated with their registered address.

**Role-Based Access Control (RBAC).** Refers to the access control method that ReCARES implemented to restrict the information and functions of the system based on a user's assigned role. Sensitive information regarding cases of VAWC and child welfare will only be accessible to authorized personnel while routine transactions will be routed to general access areas.

**Security.** Refers to one of the quality characteristics under the ISO/IEC 25010 Software Quality Model. Security evaluates how effectively ReCARES protects information and data that may be accessed by Residents, HOA Staff, and Security Personnel in accordance with each user's level of authorization.

**Security Personnel.** Refers to the on-duty guards stationed at the subdivision checkpoint and perimeter who are responsible for monitoring subdivision safety and responding to the discreet emergency alert transmitted via ReCARES. In this study, Security Personnel form one of the platform's defined user roles and are restricted to the alert's location and timestamp, with no access to a resident's household case history or to any report classified under Republic Act No. 9262 or Republic Act No. 7610.

**Subdivision Phase.** Refers to the sectional division used within Camella Homes Tibig to organize addresses. ReCARES uses the subdivision phase to prefill location data for registered residents activating an emergency alert.

**System Admin.** Refers to the role that assigns roles and maintains system configuration, classification suggestion rules, and the audit log. In this study the System Admin cannot open report text, case narratives, or alert contents.

**System Usability Scale (SUS).** Refers to the standardized questionnaire instrument used in this study to assess ReCARES's usability during User Acceptance Testing; the SUS will be administered to Security Personnel, Residents (including residents with disabilities or mobility limitations), and HOA Staff.

**Trusted person.** Refers to one adult named by a resident to submit routine documents and service requests on that resident's behalf. In this study the trusted person cannot file a confidential report and cannot register a device for another adult.

**User Acceptance Testing (UAT).** Refers to the evaluation process through which the sample defined in Section 3.6, including residents with disabilities or mobility limitations, will test the ReCARES platform prior to its turnover; the platform's usability will be evaluated using the SUS against the success thresholds in that section.

**Violence Against Women and Children (VAWC).** Refers to any act of gender-based violence resulting in physical, sexual, or psychological harm to women or children. VAWC may occur in public or within the home. VAWC is formally defined in accordance with the United Nations Declaration on the Elimination of Violence Against Women and is addressed in this study by ReCARES's objective of discreetly reporting VAWC.

**Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG).** Refers to the internationally referenced standard for evaluating and designing accessible web content. In this study WCAG is cited as the most frequently referenced accessibility standard in the literature regarding eGovernment web portal accessibility for people with disabilities.

This chapter examines the literature and other studies that support the development of ReCARES, a web-based platform proposed for Camella Homes Tibig Homeowners Association. The discussion proceeds across four related areas. These include violence against women and children as a legal and enforcement problem, digital accessibility as it concerns persons with disabilities, and existing Philippine systems for reporting and case management. The final area addresses the ethical boundaries that govern artificial intelligence assisted categorization in sensitive systems, a consideration directly relevant to the system design.

The sources used in this chapter have all been published in the last nine years, which complies with the recency requirement enforced throughout the paper. Older sources are included only in cases where they represent the legal groundwork, preserving their relevance regardless of the date of publication.

## **2.1 Review of Related Literature**

### **Violence Against Women and Children Legal Frameworks**

The issue of violence against women and children has been regarded as a violation of human rights, according to experts in the field. However, they disagree on how well various countries have converted this concern into the actual protection of the victims. The 1993 United Nations Declaration on the Elimination of Violence Against Women made this recognition formally by defining violence against women as gender-related violence causing physical as well as psychological harm if committed in any form and at any place (as cited in Consignado et al., 2022). Karunan and Singh (2017) observe that this recognition is shared around the Asia-Pacific region. The authors note that even the countries that have adopted new legal acts to address violence are yet to fight the normalization of the issue in different contexts, especially after the UN Report on Violence against Children released in 2006. Willie et al. (2022) also note that the high incidence of intimate partner violence as reported in countries across the Asia-Pacific region is linked to gender stereotypes rather than to poverty or educational status.

The Philippines has reacted to the above issue through legislative action. The Republic Act 9262 known as the Anti-Violence Against Women and Children Act has defined violence perpetrated against women and children by their spouses, partners, or parents and has created an obligation for the local government units to set up assistance mechanisms for victims (Republic of the Philippines, 2004). The Republic Act 9710 known as the Magna Carta of Women requires the establishment of a women’s desk in each barangay in the Philippines (as cited in Consignado et al., 2022). The Republic Act 7610 known as the Special Protection of Children Against Abuse, Exploitation and Discrimination Act extends the same protection to children (Republic of the Philippines, 1992). Thus, in theory, this system functions perfectly; however, Valdez et al. (2022) find that it does not function in practice, and the reasons for gender violence in the country may be traced back to the patriarchal attitudes of family structures and scattered referral routes for healthcare, legal, and social services. At the same time, the researchers note that the judgment procedures in the Philippines contribute to the choice of putting the issue of violence on hold.

The research conducted by Consignado et al. (2022) revealed that the desk officers of the barangay violence against women desks have no educational qualifications or age requirements. The desk officers react to incoming complaints and do not apply preventive measures. In some cases, the officers conduct informal mediation between the perpetrators and the victims of violence despite the fact that such practices are strictly prohibited by the law (Republic Act 9262). Clores et al. (2018) researched the area of Malvar, Batangas, and found that victims of domestic violence tolerated the violence they suffered for a significant amount of time because they were thinking of their children and not themselves. In other words, in their interviews, the two interviewed women said that it was the situation with their children that initiated them reporting the cases of domestic violence against them.

It was shown by Labrague et al. (2022) that the situation in Samar was the same, which shows that there is a similar social trend in the Philippines rather than just a local practice. Mendoza (2025) makes an important observation regarding the service provided by the officers in four municipalities in the Laguna Province. The author explains that the self evaluation of the quality of the service was not a reliable predictor of how effective the work of the officers in different areas (confidentiality, case documentation, intervention in cases of abuse). Nevertheless, not all studies support the same thesis. Thus, Gonzalez et al. (2022) revealed that the awareness of Republic Act 9262 varies depending on education and civil status of the respondents (n=227). The mentioned literature outlines the major implications for designing the system. Although the Philippine legislation stipulates that the system of legal measures can be implemented, it still relies on the officers’ training that is not the same in the various parts of the country (not all officers are qualified). Residents should have a way of reporting about their problems without the fear of being appraised and mentioned in their locality, and it can be achieved with the help of the emergency alert feature.

### **PWD Accessibility and Digital Inclusion**

The Magna Carta for Disabled Persons, Republic Act 7277 mandates the responsibility of Philippine institutions to provide services that can be utilized. However, studies reveal the great disparity in the level of compliance with this requirement. According to Ouaziz et al. (2026), the issue of website accessibility for disabled people should be considered a global research problem which has not been resolved yet, as the authors have conducted a systematic review of 112 works related to government accessibility published from 2000 to 2025. The relevant research conducted by Frandell and Allgood (2026) examines the American local government websites and demonstrates that the accessibility result can be improved in time by continual monitoring instead of one-time compliance. The researchers Jordanoski and Nielsen (2023) also highlight the reduction of compliance of the websites in low-income countries, as compared to high- and middle-income countries.

Mangku (2021) has researched the legal protection of disabled women and children in Indonesia and concluded that in theory, they are given protection according to the law but in practice, implementation of the law is not happening in the country. The same gap may be found in the Philippines as well. Majid (2026) has evaluated government electronic systems for disabled persons in Indonesia and the Philippines and proven that these systems are not easily navigated in the Philippines. The above-listed studies prove the need for accessibility channels in the provision of services.

### **Digital Tools for VAWC Reporting and Incident Case Management**

The finding regarding digital reporting indicates two interrelated aspects: evidence that these mechanisms are effective along with evidence that none has been able to pilot them in the context of the current study. Thus, Mortel and Balahadia (2019) presented HELP2JUANA, a portal for Laguna that combines electronic reporting and incident mapping. The design of the system was based on the Agile approach. A victim can report an incident using HELP2JUANA and see exactly where it took place on the map, which provided the first-ever location context to responders that traditional paper logs could not offer. Balahadia et al. (2022) developed the relevant mobile application in Laguna and discovered that the victim found its confidentiality service to be beneficial in terms of protection from monitoring by the partner. Similarly, Subiza et al. (2026) designed an online complaint submission system targeting barangay desks, which allowed routing complaints directly to the emergency hotlines.

Despite the similarities in their purpose and functionalities, each of the discussed systems cannot be used in subdivisions governed by the homeowners’ association; they were developed within the framework of the barangay or provincial government. They never included artificial intelligence-based machine categorization. This function has been provided by Cruz and Balahadia (2023), who used Latent Dirichlet Allocation, a statistical technique for document categorization by topics, for the analysis of the legislative documentation of Laguna.

In summary, the mentioned systems helped define the necessary set of design features of the system based on its functionalities: mapped reporting, privacy-protecting application, direct routing of pertinent complaints, and topic categorization. However, none of the systems is able to combine all these functions under the homeowners’ association’s regulations.

### **AI-Assisted Categorization and Ethical Boundaries**

According to Das (2025), the issue of accessibility rights in artificial intelligence should be regarded as a design structural issue rather than an individual-related issue, with assistive technology viewed as covered by Article 9 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. The author notes that the risks related to algorithm-based bias and data protection grow when AI models get too much independence in a system that supposedly assists vulnerable individuals. Such concerns are relevant to the system that functions based on sensitive case information because misclassification might lead to dire consequences for the abused individual. Cruz and Balahadia (2023) refer to the same caution in practice, where the automated tool gathers files on the topic but leaves all further decisions to humans.

The relevant cluster serves to define the limits of the system’s AI functionality. It suggests a category. It does not link case files, does not choose a queue, does not make decisions, and does not connect to the case database. Application code performs the household lookup. Routing follows the report type the resident selects. This point is important as, by virtue of Republic Act 9262 and Republic Act 7610, referral to the proper office is the Case Handler's task, and that role does not replace the barangay, the police, or emergency services.

### **Foreign Related Studies**

International research on gender-based violence indicates that cultural and structural conditions sustain intimate partner violence in ways that cannot be addressed only by legal or technological measures. Willie et al. (2022) identify this trend in gender bias attitudes in the Asia-Pacific region. Along similar lines, Bhamani et al. (2023) point to dowry practices specific to Pakistan. In their research, Vyas et al. (2023) broaden this issue by carrying out a study in seven countries (India, Nepal, Pakistan, Cambodia, the Philippines, Timor-Leste, and Vietnam). Because of the inclusion of the Philippines in the research, the current study is one of the closest foreign examples available. Further evidence of the topic can be provided by Karunan and Singh (2017), who conducted research to trace the patterns of social normalization in the Asia-Pacific region.

Two other strands of international research enrich this picture. Koirala and Chuemchit (2020) conducted a review of thirty-eight studies paving the way to the conclusion about the interconnection of postpartum depression and domestic violence. Wardhani and Christia (2020) investigated instances of physical violence against domestic workers in Indonesia to come up with the conclusion about the weakness of legal protection despite the economic development of the country. In terms of child protection, Salampessy et al. (2022) analyzed the issues of the implementation of the Convention on the Rights of the Child in the Southeast Asian region. Raman et al. (2020) argue that there is a need for concerted effort across the health care, judicial, and social protection systems in order to stop violence against children. Juraev (2022) emphasizes that domestic violence research in Central Asia is suffering from the inconsistency of data processing, which leads to the conclusion that the development of the digital form should be preceded by the collection of data in a consistent manner.

The aforementioned authors are focused on the accessibility aspect of the issue. As per Ouaziz et al. (2026) and Frandell and Allgood (2026), digital accessibility indicates an unsolved research issue across the globe. Jordanoski and Nielsen (2023) contributed a comparative perspective on the matter by illustrating the decline in compliance along with decreasing income levels of the countries. In the research conducted by Abdurahman and Kabanda (2024), interviews with the public sector employees of South Africa resulted in the conclusion that accessible designs rely on organizational infrastructure and stakeholder buy-in being in place.

Patan (2024) and Alekseev et al. (2025) are also interested in accessible design from the more technical point of view. Both authors provide analysis of practical tool-kits available for such designs like semantic HTML, assistive roles, and adaptive methodologies for making interfaces adaptable. In the research done by Atata (2025), it has been argued that accessibility should be taken into account in user interface prototyping at the earliest stages of designing. Zhuang et al. (2025) are reading documentation related to the national apps of digital identity and mention that disabled users are subject to specific risks of exclusion from the process of governmental identification.

### **Local Related Studies**

Philippine studies showcase the best evidence base for this particular project showing the enforcement gap that this system is dealing with on national and community levels. Mission (2024) utilized a density-based clustering algorithm on 248 cases of Republic Act 9262 documented by Philippine National Police in Antique province during 2018–2023, which were mapped using geographic information system and discovered that the number of cases was growing year by year and had some localized geographic spots in some years despite the fact that the clustering index of the province showed that in general there was no statistically significant pattern. Kibtiah and Tuharea (2024) came up with a complementary finding using data of Philippine Statistics Authority from 2010 to 2020. They used Heise’s ecological framework to indicate that situational factors, such as male dominance and financial control in the family, are stronger triggers of violence against women than individual or social factors. This study also reflects the impact of financial dependency and social shame on victims who do not file reports even when there is a proper system to do so.

The most pertinent studies regarding the barangay desk in relation to its shortcomings were conducted by Consignado et al. (2022) and Mendoza (2025) with particular emphasis on the role of the qualifications of desk officers, the nature of procedural compliance, and the credibility of self-assessment done by the officers compared to actual service performance. According to the multi-dimensional study of the violence against women desks conducted in Calamba City by Consignado et al. (2022), the desk officers seemingly lack any applicable rules regarding qualifying age as well as the educational qualifications. It also appears that these officers take action only once the issues arise rather than working to prevent them. Moreover, considering the effectual evidence of failure to comply with existing legal norms, some of the officers had continued to operate as mediators even after the achievability of this operation was prohibited by RA 9262. While it is clear that these desks were operational, it does not correspond to their intended function. According to Clores et al. (2018) and Labrague et al. (2022), the researchers managed to detect forms of delayed reporting in various provinces. In the course of her research, Clores (1998) managed to review the booklet records of the Women and Children Protection Desk in Malvar, Batangas, covering the years from 2014 to 2016, as a result of which thirty cases were identified in the destination area despite the decree of a decreased prevalence of other forms of abuse in the same area. In particular, both victims whose cases were reviewed reported them only after they weighed in their children's safety or lack thereof as well as after they recognized that the abuser had some issues with alcohol.

The studies by Valdez et al. (2022) and Gonzalez et al. (2022) give statistics related to aid-seeking behavior for the whole country, including data about reporting gaps and the distribution of knowledge in different barangays. In terms of technologies, Mortel and Balahadia (2019) and Balahadia et al. (2022) developed the first prototype of the use of digitalization in the Philippines while Subiza et al. (2026) laid foundations for the routing of complaints to call centers in remote communities. Cruz and Balahadia (2023) illustrate the example of using automated classification in public authorities by means of applying Latent Dirichlet Allocation to the records of provincial legislative offices. The aforementioned situation highlights that the issue is systemic, rather than municipal; however, the modern utilities refer only to barangays or provincial offices, thus indicating the governance gap that is accomplished by ReCARES.

## **Synthesis**

The literature reviewed above reveals a commonality between four related areas. The law of the Philippines recognizes the problems that this system solves, but methods used for achieving that have repeatedly failed at the point of contact. Republic Act 9262, Republic Act 9710 and Republic Act 7610 have created a legally valid framework, whereas there are officer-operated desks lacking privacy (Consignado et al. 2022; Valdez et al., 2022). The analysis carried out by Ouaziz et al. (2026) and Majid (2026) shows that the same failure occurs when it comes to residents with disabilities on a global scale and in the Philippines. The study conducted by Mortel and Balahadia (2019) proves that digital reporting does work, but not yet at subdivisions governed by homeowners’ associations. Das (2025) sets the ethical limit by banning artificial intelligence from making decisions on its own and keeping categorization possibilities only.

There are two key problems arising from the literature. Legal and effective protection is there, but it does not help the people it was designed for due to problems with barangay delivery. The literature looks into the existence of digital solutions to address these problems, but they exist in the barangay and provincial government setting, without the design being used in the homeowners’ association structure. ReCARES is proposed as a solution to both problems analyzed above.

Based on the synthesis, the system should provide a discreet, GPS-tagged emergency button, so that the residents are able to report the incident without anyone noticing the report filing. It should have available digital services that do not require any visits to the relevant office, in order to deal with accessibility problems. An artificial intelligence module would suggest a category only. Application code would link a confidential report to the household file and would route that report because the resident chose the confidential type. That split keeps categorization assistance without giving the model a queue, a database lookup, or an outcome.

# **Chapter 3: Research Methodology**

This chapter presents the research design, software development method, data gathering instruments, procedural phases, technical architecture, and evaluation framework that will govern the construction of ReCARES. This chapter outlines the existing service gaps that have been observed at Camella Homes Tibig, namely passing of information concerning incidents through a personal Facebook account, lack of a private channel for reporting abuse incidents, and a walk-in-only approach which deprives people with mobility issues of the opportunity to use the service, along with the project development requirements defined on the basis of existing publications on web-based violence against women and children reporting, role-based access system, and accessible e-government services system. The chapter is organized into six sections: Research Design, Research Method, Data Gathering Instruments, Data Gathering Procedure, Technical Background and System Architecture, and System Evaluation.

## **3.1 Research Design**

The study will adopt Design Science Research (DSR) rather than a purely descriptive or evaluative design, because observing and describing Camella Homes Tibig's current operations can confirm that a gap exists but cannot establish whether a proposed alternative actually closes it once real residents, security personnel, and staff interact with it. A descriptive study stops at diagnosis. DSR will be adopted specifically because it treats the platform itself as the research instrument. In this manner, ReCARES will not be an outcome of the study but the means for achieving its purpose, including incidental reporting and digital access to services.

The choice is further supported by what the two academic objectives are actually claims about. A discreet VAWC reporting channel and an accessible PWD service channel are both claims about lived experience under specific constraints, namely the fear of being seen reporting abuse, physical mobility limits at a single fixed-hours office, and dependence on an SOS mechanism working correctly under real emergency conditions. None of these constraints can be verified through document review or interview data alone, since a resident's account of what she would do in an emergency is not the same evidence as observing whether the alert she actually triggers reaches a responder. DSR is the design built specifically to close that gap, since it requires the artifact to be built and then tested against the same residents and conditions that produced the operational gaps described above, rather than accepting a well-argued design as sufficient proof on its own. No respondents have been engaged and no data have been gathered as of the writing of this proposal; both will begin only once this methodology has been reviewed and approved.

The study will follow the six phases of the Peffers et al. (2007) process. Each phase is adopted here for a reason specific to this study rather than as a procedural formality: Problem Identification and Motivation is grounded in the walk-in service model and Facebook-based communication already observed at Camella Homes Tibig, so the problems this study will address are the ones actually documented on-site rather than assumed. Definition of the Objectives for a Solution will convert the study's objectives into the concept areas requiring analysis, namely security and role-based access control, reporting protocols, geolocation-based data capture, automated categorization, and web accessibility standards, so that every design decision in Section 3.2 traces back to a stated objective rather than to convenience. Design and Development, Demonstration, and Evaluation will be carried out through the Agile method and the ISO/IEC 25010 and System Usability Scale instruments described in Sections 3.2 and 3.6, chosen because a capstone timeline of one academic year does not allow for a single, monolithic build-then-test cycle without an unacceptable risk of discovering a fundamental mismatch too late to correct it. Communication will close the cycle by documenting the artifact's design rationale and evaluation results for the Homeowners Association and for future capstone groups working in similar community governance settings, since a research artifact that is not documented in a form others can act on does not satisfy DSR's own definition of a completed cycle.

A mixed-methods approach will be used within this DSR structure because the study's evidence needs are not uniform across its objectives. Qualitative data will be gathered during the needs assessment described in Section 3.4, from respondents to be drawn from residents, security personnel, and administrative staff within Camella Homes Tibig, because the barriers already identified at Camella Homes Tibig, such as a victim's reluctance to be seen entering a VAW desk, are matters of judgment and circumstance that a numeric scale cannot capture on its own. Quantitative data will be gathered during the formal evaluation phase using the instruments in Section 3.3, once the platform has been built and is ready for testing, because a capstone defense requires evidence that the platform's quality and usability can be measured and compared against a standard rather than asserted. Relying on either method alone would leave one of the two claims this study makes, that the system will be designed around real operational friction and that it will perform acceptably once built, without adequate support.

## **3.2 Research Method**

In order to make ReCARES, the proponents will use the Agile Software Development Life Cycle, rather than the Waterfall or plan-driven model. This is because the system will be used to automate and digitalize a process related to people that the developers can’t fully control. The routing logic of the Case Handler or the formulation of the alert confirmation can be modified based on the feedback about the needs of the system, and the implementation of the Waterfall methodology that implies the completion of all specifications at the very beginning will not allow developers to make changes regarding the system’s functionality after implementing the system. The proponents will split the development of the system into six phases based on the common Agile cycle: Planning, Requirements Analysis, Design, Implementation, Testing, and Maintenance.

**Phase 1: Planning.** The objective of this stage is to clarify the goals of the platform, establish its stakeholders, and outline the project scope before any programming work starts. The stakeholders in this project will be six user types, namely Resident, Security Personnel, Homeowners Association Staff, Case Handler, Homeowners Association Admin, and System Admin. In this stage, the proponents will clarify the types of incidents and service requests that will be supported by the software in the course of a single academic year. The phase of planning will also determine the main priorities of the system such as reliability of discreet alerts and confidentiality of VAWC and child welfare data, before any decisions regarding interface or database design.

**Phase 2: Requirements Analysis.** The information obtained through the Needs Assessment Questionnaire and Interview Guide in Section 3.3 will be transformed into functional and non-functional requirements once data gathering commences. Functional requirements involve capturing the location when an SOS alert is triggered, role-based authentication, routing that follows the report type the resident chooses, an AI category suggestion that does not choose the queue, household linking performed by application code, a trusted adult limited to routine requests, device registration by each adult for their own device, and an online workflow for requesting and tracking a service. Non-functional requirements will cover row-level database security, an auditable submission history, separation of the AI suggestion from both routing and the database, processing of personal information under Republic Act No. 10173, and reliability of the discreet alert. All of the above requirements will be recorded in the Software Requirements Specification before starting the Design.

**Phase 3: Design.** This stage will establish the architecture of the platform, the schema of the database, and the interface configuration of the platform, which will be elaborated in section 3.5. The construction will be done in such a way that the most high-risk capabilities such as resident/user authentication and the role access control will be developed and validated in the fastest period of time because the rest of the functionalities including SOS alerts and AI classifier will depend on the access limitations working properly. Fixing a defect which will be spotted in that stage of the development process would take much more resources than fixing it in the earlier stage.

**Phase 4: Implementation.** Development and integration processes will take place in iterations of two weeks, which is the timeframe for the team to actually obtain feedback on their work: the sprints have to be short enough to allow for the completion of each increment during a single advisor meeting, but long enough for a three-person team to develop one complete testable feature without splitting one feature into parts whose development will span multiple sprints. During the earliest sprints, the team will work on authentication, assignment of user roles, and registration of users. Later sprints will add other functionalities related to document submission, routing, tracking status of the work done, and the layer of artificial intelligence-based categorization after the relevant access controls have been put in place and tested.

**Phase 5: Testing.** Testing will be segmented into two comprehensive stages instead of relying on a single test run, as solely relying on tests conducted by the developers cannot demonstrate whether the reporting system is actually trustworthy and usable for the relevant users. Developer Functional Testing will take place after Developer Testing and is handled internally by using the System Testing Checklist in Section 3.3, which will be used to ensure that the system works properly when presented with both favorable and negative input before being released publicly. User Acceptance Testing will take place as a separate stage and not be integrated with functional testing because it is not possible for the developers to make judgments on behalf of residents, security personnel, and Homeowners Association staff about acceptability.

**Phase 6: Maintenance.** Maintenance shall be limited mainly to error rectification and interface modifications based on the findings of User Acceptance Testing. Any additional maintenance would require commitment from the proponents to support the long-term deployment of the capstone project at Camella Homes Tibig, which is also outside of the completion timeframe established for this study.

## **3.3 Data Gathering Instruments**

The five instruments employed by this study will be matched to each phase of the DSR and Agile procedures, as they will be implemented. No instruments have been administered; below is the order of implementation as data collection commences.

The **Needs Assessment Questionnaire and Interview Guide** is the instrument developed by a researcher that consists of structured questionnaire items, coupled with additional open-ended interview prompts. It will be used prior to beginning the actual development of the hardware/software system based upon data collected from individuals, including the residents, security personnel or staff from the Homeowners Association, of Camella Homes Tibig, to ascertain the Walk In Procedures currently in place, delays with regards to manual logging entries and determine specific scenarios where existing channels for reporting are not appropriate. The information derived from this will assist in identifying the functional and non-Functional Requirements incorporated into the initial sprints discussed in Section 3.2.

The **Software Requirements Validation Checklist** is based on the Software Requirements Specification that was produced during the Requirements Analysis. During the development walkthroughs, the checklist will be used by the proponents of the study and the adviser to validate that all required components, including the features, database tables, and role-based permissions, have been delivered according to specification prior to beginning the next sprint.

The **System Testing Checklist** serves as a checklist for the proponents to use during Developer Functional Testing to ensure the functionality of the system. The checklist will include tests for the following areas: authentication; access control boundaries between the six roles, including a check that the System Admin cannot open report content; transmission of SOS alert messages with location and timestamp and without report text; reliability of repeated alert submission; a check that AI output is only a suggested category and that confidential routing follows the resident's chosen report type; and household linking performed by application code. The checklist will also include tests to verify that the system interfaces conform to the success criteria of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines adopted during design.

The **ISO/IEC 25010 Evaluation Questionnaire** is a structured questionnaire that was based upon the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model (International Organization for Standardization, 2011). Respondents' agreement with the five quality characteristics being evaluated in this research (functional suitability, performance efficiency, usability, security, and reliability) will be measured using a four-point Likert scale where one represents strong disagreement and four represents strong agreement.

The **System Usability Scale** was created by Brooke (1996), it is a standardized instrument containing ten items that measure perceived ease of use, satisfaction, and learnability. Each respondent will provide a score for each item on a five-point rating scale; the raw scores will be combined into a single composite score ranging from 0-100 using the process outlined in Section 3.6.

## **3.4 Data Gathering Procedure**

Until the approval of this methodology, there has been no gathering of data. Once this methodology is approved, there will be four distinct, subsequent stages of data gathering as outlined in Section 3.2, which correspond to Planning, Design, Implementation and Testing.

**Stage 1: Needs Assessment.** The proponents will distribute the Needs Assessment Questionnaire and conduct interviews with selected residents, security personnel, and Homeowners Association staff at Camella Homes Tibig, after obtaining informed consent from each participant. The resident group will include residents with disabilities or mobility limitations, because the accessible-service objective cannot be specified from the accounts of residents who can already reach the office. Participants will be selected from the subdivision itself so that the findings reflect this setting. The proponents will analyze results to verify the drawbacks previously discovered in the course of study and to come up with the functional requirements assigned to each of the six user roles.

**Stage 2: Design Walkthroughs.** The proponents will conduct regular walkthroughs with the adviser throughout both Design and Implementation. These walkthroughs are designed to compare the developing interface, service request working process and administrative dashboard to the operation documentation created in Stage 1. The purpose is to provide the opportunity to assess how well the solution is working or what changes may need to be made, rather than to conduct a formal assessment at completion of each phase.

**Stage 3: System Testing.** After all of the features from a given sprint have been integrated into the final product, the system will use an environment for the System Testing Checklist that mimics the production environment but is controlled by a controlled site with sample data, and by using sample accounts for both of those options. The system will be tested to find out if it is functioning properly on its own; therefore a user should enter incomplete or invalid information into the system, which is being defined and agreed upon by the User Acceptance Testing team, to make sure the system is performing correctly and that the validation requirements for both roles and for the user will be satisfied before the build can proceed to User Acceptance Testing.

**Stage 4: User Acceptance Testing and Evaluation.** The User Acceptance Testing sample is 30 participants from Camella Homes Tibig: 15 residents, of whom at least 5 have a disability or a mobility limitation; 8 security personnel; and 7 Homeowners Association staff. They will perform the tasks expected of their role, including activating an SOS alert, submitting a service request, naming a trusted person for a routine request, registering their own device, and checking the status of a submitted service request. The designated Case Handler and the backup Case Handler will complete the confidential intake-and-referral tasks in a separate session, because those tasks are not assigned to general staff and are not part of the SUS sample. After the session, the 30 participants will fill out the ISO/IEC 25010 Evaluation Questionnaire and the System Usability Scale. Thresholds for a successful result are stated in Section 3.6.

## **3.5 Technical Background and System Architecture**

ReCARES is a role-based, client-server web application accessible through standard desktop and mobile browsers. The entire system will be divided into three layers instead of treating the artificial intelligence part separately, which reflects its purpose in a proper way; namely the categorization function will be included in the processing pipeline of the application layer and thus will not have the ability to function separately from the system itself and directly provide the services to the users.

**Presentation Layer.** This layer will consist of that web-based interface that will be used by the Residents, Security Personnel, and Homeowners Association staff and administrators. It will offer the SOS alert interface, the form for submitting requests of incidents or services, the view for tracking the status of the operations, the Security Personnel's alert acknowledgment view, and the dashboard for administration functions.

**Application Layer.** This layer will include the logic on the server side, involving authentication, role-based access, routing, and status changes. The layer will be constructed with Next.js 14 App Router for the server side, React 18 for the client side, and Node.js 20 LTS for the runtime. Role-based access limits each account to its assigned role. The artificial intelligence component sits in this pipeline only as a suggestion: it reads the text fields of a report and returns a suggested category. It does not query the database, does not link records, and does not choose a queue. Application code compares the household on a confidential report with open case files and links a match. Routing follows the report type the resident chose. The confidential type goes to the Case Handler. Every other type goes to Homeowners Association Staff, even if the suggestion names a confidential category. Security Personnel receive an SOS alert's location and timestamp only, for as long as it takes to dispatch a response, with no report text, category, or household history. The Case Handler records intake and a referral to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911, and does not mediate. The System Admin cannot open report bodies. Personal data is processed under Republic Act No. 10173: collection is limited to the purpose of the alert, report, or request, access follows the role, and each opening of a confidential file is written to the audit log.

**Data Layer.** Data concerning the system will be stored in a Supabase-managed PostgreSQL database, which will include row-level security such that access limits hold at the database as well as in the application. The database will include user accounts and roles, incident and service request records, case files, status history, device registrations tied to the adult who registered them, the named trusted person for routine requests, and monthly counts of complaints by phase. Those monthly counts are compiled by the application from report totals. The AI suggestion is not an input to that count, and the summary does not include case narrative.

Moreover, the system will require a stable Internet connection for both access for residents and administrative control and it will never function on an offline basis. As a result, there will be network communication conducted via HTTPS, and authentication and validation of sessions will be conducted by the server to prevent unauthorized access to VAWC records and any other child welfare documentation during the transfer process.

**Peopleware.** Six roles use ReCARES, and the access boundary of each role stays distinct. The Resident submits incident and service requests, activates the SOS alert from a device that same adult registered, names at most one trusted adult for routine requests, and tracks their own submissions. Security Personnel receive and acknowledge the SOS alert, see only its location and timestamp, and log the response, with no household case history and no confidential report. Homeowners Association Staff act on routine service requests and documents. The Case Handler is the only role that can read a report the resident sent as confidential. The HOA board designates that handler. The handler must have training on Republic Act No. 9262 and Republic Act No. 7610. A named backup handler receives a file when the primary handler has a conflict of interest, which includes a report about the handler's own household or a relative. The handler's action is intake and referral to the Barangay VAW Desk, the PNP Women and Children Protection Desk, or 911. The handler does not mediate and does not record a legal outcome. The Homeowners Association Admin verifies residents remotely from the uploaded proof of residency and government ID, manages staff accounts, and reads the monthly aggregate counts. The System Admin manages configuration and role assignment and cannot open report content.

## **3.6 System Evaluation**

System evaluation will determine whether ReCARES achieves the two evaluation aims this study sets for itself: adherence to the ISO/IEC 25010 software quality model, and acceptable usability as measured through User Acceptance Testing. No evaluation data currently exist; both instruments will be administered only after the platform has been built and User Acceptance Testing sessions have been conducted with respondents from within the subdivision.

**ISO/IEC 25010 Evaluation.** The proponents will evaluate ReCARES against five characteristics. Functional suitability will assess whether the platform provides its core functions, including SOS alert activation, role-based access control, a category suggestion that does not change routing, application-side household linking, Case Handler referral, and the digital service request workflow. Performance efficiency will assess response time and resource use under expected resident and administrative load. Usability will assess whether residents, including residents with disabilities or mobility limitations, security personnel, and staff can operate the platform with reasonable effort after a brief orientation. Security will assess whether role-based access and row-level database security keep a confidential report with the Case Handler, keep report text off the security view, and keep report content away from the System Admin. Reliability will assess whether the discreet alert completes under repeated activation and still delivers location and timestamp with no report text. A result meets the aim when each characteristic reaches a weighted mean of at least 3.26, which Table 2 interprets as Strongly Agree. In developer functional testing, the alert checklist passes only when every trial delivers that payload and none drops the submission. Table 2 presents the four-point Likert interpretation that will be applied to questionnaire responses once they have been collected.

| --- | --- |
| 3.26–4.00 | Strongly Agree |
| 2.51–3.25 | Agree |
| 1.76–2.50 | Disagree |
| 1.00–1.75 | Strongly Disagree |

*Table 2. Four-Point Likert Scale Interpretation for the ISO/IEC 25010 Evaluation*

**System Usability Scale Scoring.** Following Brooke (1996), respondents will score each of the ten System Usability Scale items on a five-point scale from one, strongly disagree, to five, strongly agree. For odd-numbered items, the score contribution will be the raw response minus one. For even-numbered items, the score contribution will be five minus the raw response. The ten contributions will be summed, producing a value between zero and forty, and the sum will be multiplied by two and a half to produce a composite score between zero and one hundred. A higher composite score will indicate higher perceived usability. A composite score of at least 68 is the minimum acceptable usability result for this study. A score below 68 fails that aim.

**Sample.** The User Acceptance Testing sample is 30 participants from Camella Homes Tibig: 15 residents, of whom at least 5 have a disability or a mobility limitation; 8 security personnel; and 7 Homeowners Association staff. The Case Handler and the backup are not part of this sample. Their intake-and-referral tasks are covered by the developer checklist and a separate walkthrough.

**Evaluation Limitations.** The evaluation findings will reflect only the 30 participants named in the sample above, during the capstone period. Bound to the single-academic-year capstone timeline, these findings will describe the platform's performance and acceptability within that testing window and will not constitute a claim of long-term institutional adoption or of performance at a deployment scale beyond Camella Homes Tibig.
